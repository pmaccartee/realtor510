How are fire insurance constraints affecting buyer psychology?

The story on fire insurance in the East Bay hills has changed — and buyers who are still operating on two-year-old assumptions are miscalibrated in both directions.

What's actually happening now

Through 2023 and into 2024, insurance availability in high fire-risk zones was a genuine constraint. Major carriers pulled back from California hillside markets, and buyers in neighborhoods like Montclair, Claremont, the Berkeley Hills, and upper Oakland hills found themselves scrambling for coverage — sometimes at two or three times the cost they expected, sometimes through the FAIR Plan as a last resort.

That picture has started to shift. Carriers are returning. Chubb, AIG Private Client, and several specialty insurers have re-entered or expanded their appetite for well-maintained, properly hardened hillside homes. Newer entrants — including several backed by reinsurance capital that sat out the California market during peak volatility — are writing policies in zones that were effectively uninsurable 18 months ago.

The new terms are different

Availability is returning, but the economics have reset. Premiums are materially higher than pre-2020 benchmarks — in some cases 40 to 80% higher for comparable coverage. Deductibles are larger. Defensible space requirements are real and enforced at underwriting, not just at renewal. Some carriers require specific roof materials, ember-resistant vents, or documented brush clearance before they'll bind coverage.

This is the critical shift in buyer psychology: insurance is no longer a deal-killer in most cases, but it is now a line item that belongs in the underwriting conversation from day one — alongside property taxes and HOA fees — not something buyers discover at the end of escrow.

What this means for buyers

Get an insurance quote before you make an offer, not after. For hillside properties in Oakland, Berkeley, Piedmont, and Montclair, have a conversation with an independent broker who works with multiple carriers and has active experience in the East Bay hills market. Know your number before you're in contract. Surprises in this category, at this stage of the market, are avoidable.

Also understand that a well-hardened home — cleared brush, Class A roof, ember-resistant construction — commands meaningfully better insurance terms. That's relevant both when buying and when prepping a home for sale.

What this means for sellers

Document your defensible space work, your roof age and material, and any fire-hardening upgrades you've done. Buyers who are sophisticated about insurance — and increasingly they are — will ask. Having that information ready, and being able to point them toward carriers who are actively writing in your neighborhood, is a competitive advantage in a negotiation.

The insurance market is not fixed. It is stabilizing. The buyers who understand that distinction will move with confidence. The ones who don't will hesitate — or overpay for that hesitation when someone else moves first.
