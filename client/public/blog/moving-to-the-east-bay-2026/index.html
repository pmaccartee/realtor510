Moving to the East Bay in 2026: A Guide for People Leaving San Francisco

Where your budget buys a category more, your kid's school isn't a lottery, and the commute is a train, not a bridge.

You're not choosing between San Francisco and the East Bay — you've already decided to leave. The real choice is the Inner East Bay — Oakland, Berkeley, Piedmont, Alameda — versus the alternatives, and on value, schools, space, weather, the commute, and food, it wins on nearly every line.

The short version

  • Price: SF single-family median runs about $2.2M; the East Bay's is about $1.36M — roughly 38% less for the same house type.
  • Category, not discount: a median SF condo (~$1.33M) buys a detached East Bay house with a yard for about the same money.
  • Schools: most East Bay communities assign schools by home address — no San Francisco lottery, boundaries knowable before you write an offer.
  • Commute: Rockridge to the Embarcadero is about 20 minutes on BART — no toll, no bridge.
  • The catch: the list price is an opening move, not the price — it varies block by block, which is where local guidance earns its keep.

Everyone leaves for Marin first. The smart money crosses the other bridge.

Everyone leaving San Francisco looks north first. Marin is the reflex — the golden hills, the water, the glossy relocation guides. And Marin is lovely. But for most people crossing a bridge to buy, Marin is a car-dependent, single-note answer to a question the East Bay answers better on nearly every line.

Asked on CNBC's Closing Bell Overtime where he'd buy a home if he could buy anywhere in the country, Compass CEO Robert Reffkin said the East Bay. His reason: every other major Bay Area market has already climbed back past its 2022 highs, and this one hasn't yet. He's not wrong — and he's naming the least interesting reason to move here. Here's the rest of it.

You're not choosing between San Francisco and the East Bay — you've already decided to leave. The real choice is East Bay versus the alternatives: Marin, the Peninsula. And on that comparison the East Bay wins on value, schools, space, weather, the commute, and a food-and-culture scene that isn't close.

You'll actually know which school your kid attends

In most of the East Bay you buy into an attendance area and that's your school — no lottery. Talk to enough families who've made this move and a theme repeats: it isn't only about test scores, it's about knowing which school your kid will actually attend.

In San Francisco, elementary assignment runs through a lottery. You can buy a home a block from a school you love and still be placed across town. For a lot of families, that lack of control is the thing that finally tips the decision.

The East Bay works differently. Piedmont, Albany, and the Lamorinda districts operate on straightforward neighborhood assignment, and the boundaries are knowable before you write an offer. The house you choose determines the school. No lottery, no waiting, no backup plan.

Families do tend to concentrate in the best-known school communities — Piedmont, Albany, Kensington, the Lamorinda towns — and that demand shows up in pricing from cycle to cycle. It moves around, though; school-district premiums run warmer in some years and cooler in others, so treat schools as one factor in your decision rather than a permanent law of the market.

If schools matter to your search, start there. My East Bay School Guide maps ratings and attendance boundaries against neighborhood median prices, so you can see the trade-offs before you fall in love with a house.

Out here, the list price is an opening move — not the price

The single thing that catches newcomers most: out here, the list price is an opening move, not the price. It's better to hear it now than mid-search. It isn't new and it isn't a bubble — Oakland has priced this way since the 1990s, long before San Francisco picked up the habit. It simply means the number on a listing tells you very little on its own.

What matters is what homes on that particular street actually clear at, and that varies more than most people expect. In a neighborhood like Crocker Highlands, two nearly identical houses across the street from one another can settle at meaningfully different numbers. It isn't in any report you can pull — it's pattern recognition earned block by block over years.

Some of the best homes never reach a public portal at all, or trade in the few days before they do. That access isn't a search tool — it's relationships built over time with the agents who work these pockets.

Neighborhoods, decoded

Medians are single-family detached, BridgeMLS (Real Estate Decoded, July 2026) — a starting frame, not a price tag. Every one of these places has a wide range.

Rockridge$1.8M

The one that converts San Francisco buyers fastest. Craftsman bungalows on tree-lined streets, College Avenue running through the middle of it with Market Hall, the bookstores, the sidewalk tables. You can live here without touching your car, and BART puts you at the Embarcadero in about twenty minutes. If what you loved about San Francisco was walking out your door into a real neighborhood, this is where that feeling lives.

Upper Rockridge$1.8M+

Where Rockridge climbs into the hills. Wider lots, mid-century moderns with Bay views, quieter streets, and Hillcrest K–8 — one of the most sought-after schools in Oakland Unified. A little more room to breathe, a five-minute drop back down to College Avenue when you want the action.

Montclair$1.85M

Its own village tucked into the hills, with a proper little downtown of restaurants and shops. Wooded lots, big houses, deer in the yard, and a genuine sense of retreat fifteen minutes from downtown Oakland. This is where your money buys square footage and trees — the classic "we didn't know we could have this" moment for buyers coming from a city flat.

Crocker Highlands & Trestle Glen$1.7M

If you care about architecture, start here. Spanish Eclectic, Mediterranean Revival, and Tudor homes built in the 1920s and '30s, most of them lovingly kept, on curving streets that were designed to curve. Grand Lake and its farmers market on one side, Piedmont on the other. Trestle Glen is the quieter sibling just over the ridge — same beloved elementary, a little more house for the money.

Piedmont$3.3M

An independent city of its own, wrapped entirely inside Oakland — own police, own schools, own civic life. Estate homes, old oaks, and public schools that families genuinely move here for. It's the East Bay's most established address, and for a certain buyer it's the whole reason they cross the bridge.

San Francisco has a mansion shortage. Piedmont has a comeback: "We've got mansions — yes we do! We've got mansions — how 'bout you?"

Berkeley$1.74M

Craftsman architecture at its peak, the Gourmet Ghetto, Elmwood's shops, Claremont's hills, and a university that keeps the whole place intellectually caffeinated. Three BART stations, twenty-five minutes to the city. For buyers who want culture and character rather than polish, Berkeley is hard to beat at this price.

Alameda$1.45M

A flat, bikeable island with one of the most intact Victorian streetscapes in California, three ferry terminals, and a beach. Alameda Unified earned a National Blue Ribbon in 2024, which makes this a rare schools-and-value combination. Park Street for dinner, the Seaplane Lagoon ferry under twenty minutes to downtown San Francisco. Quiet luxury with actual bones.

Albany$1.575M

Small, walkable, and genuinely excellent — Albany Unified is one of the best districts in the Bay Area, and the town is charming in an unshowy way. Solano Avenue for the shops and the food, close enough to Berkeley to borrow its culture. Consistently the best value-per-school-quality trade in the region.

Kensington$1.73M

An unincorporated hillside enclave above El Cerrito with sweeping Bay views, winding roads, and Berkeley schools. Tiny, leafy, and quietly beloved by the people who live there. If you want views and calm without a long drive, this is the pocket.

El Cerrito$1.2M

The value story right now. Two BART stations, postwar bungalows and hillside homes with views, an easy family pace, and prices meaningfully below its neighbors. If your budget is the binding constraint, this is where it stretches furthest without giving up location.

Orinda, Lafayette & Moraga~$2M

Just through the Caldecott: big lots, mature trees, a suburban rhythm, and the Acalanes Union district — among the strongest public schools in California. This is the choice for families who want space and certainty and don't mind a fifteen-minute drive to reach it.

A note on Oakland: the citywide median sits around $1.0M, but Oakland is not one market. The range across its neighborhoods is enormous — which is exactly why block-level guidance matters more than any citywide number. See the full Oakland neighborhood guide.

Marin means a bridge. The East Bay means a train.

The East Bay commute is a train, not a bridge — and that's the whole difference. The biggest myth is that Marin means a better commute. In reality, Marin means a car and a bridge that doesn't always cooperate. From Rockridge, you're about twenty minutes from the Embarcadero by BART — no toll, no merge, no white-knuckling the approach. Berkeley, 25–27 minutes. El Cerrito, about 30. The Alameda–Oakland ferry runs a genuinely civilized route to the Ferry Building.

With hybrid work now permanent for most professionals, the math has shifted further. When you're heading in two or three days a week, the question isn't whether the commute is manageable — it's whether the lifestyle you're buying is worth the price. On that calculation, the East Bay wins decisively.

Five reasons people leave SF for the Inner East Bay

  • Value — your budget buys a category more: detached instead of attached, a yard instead of a balcony, architecture instead of a footprint.
  • Schools — Piedmont, Albany, Berkeley, and Lamorinda rank among the state's best, with deep private options layered on top. See the School Guide.
  • Space & yards — the thing an SF condo can't give you and a Marin house makes you drive everywhere to get.
  • Weather — most of the East Bay's best neighborhoods get dramatically more sun than their foggy SF counterparts.
  • Food & culture — College Avenue, Temescal, Lakeshore, Piedmont Avenue, the Gourmet Ghetto. One of the strongest food scenes in the country.

Should you make the move?

If you want space, sun, schools, a real food scene, and a train that gets you to the city faster than a Marin driveway gets you to the bridge — yes. If your heart is set on a specific slice of San Francisco that has no equivalent anywhere, maybe not. The honest answer depends on what you actually love about where you live now. That's the first conversation, and it's the one I start every relocation with.

Frequently asked

Is the East Bay cheaper than San Francisco?
In specific neighborhoods, meaningfully. The regional single-family median is about $1.36M, and your budget generally buys a category more — detached instead of attached, a yard, a garage. The best of Piedmont, Rockridge, and the Berkeley hills still out-price plenty of San Francisco, so value lives in specific pockets rather than a blanket discount.
How long is the commute to San Francisco?
By BART: Rockridge about 20 minutes, Berkeley 25–27, El Cerrito about 30 — all to the Embarcadero, with no toll or bridge. Ferries from Alameda and Jack London Square run roughly 20–25 minutes to the Ferry Building.
Which East Bay cities have the best schools?
Piedmont Unified, Albany Unified, and Berkeley Unified are consistently top-ranked, with Lamorinda's Acalanes Union district among the best in California. Unlike San Francisco's lottery, most East Bay communities assign schools by attendance area, so the boundaries are knowable before you write an offer. See the East Bay School Guide.
What's the best East Bay neighborhood for someone leaving San Francisco?
It depends on what you loved about your San Francisco neighborhood — most SF neighborhoods have a distinct East Bay counterpart with more space and a lower price. Rockridge, Berkeley, Alameda, Piedmont, and Montclair each suit a different SF buyer.
Should I buy or sell first when relocating from SF?
It depends on your equity, financing, and tolerance for briefly carrying two mortgages. The right move is to coordinate both as one transaction rather than two disconnected events — the first thing we map out together.
